Every woman should be able to prevent cervical cancer. Not every county makes it possible.

This atlas maps the barriers that stand between women and cervical cancer prevention: missed screening, no nearby OB/GYN, no insurance, and long distances. It shows where those barriers pile up, so health departments, funders and communities can act where it matters most.

01 · Why it matters

One of the most preventable cancers, still taking thousands of lives

About 13,100 women in the United States are diagnosed with cervical cancer each year, and about 4,200 die from it. Nearly all of these cases are preventable. Whether prevention reaches a woman depends heavily on where she lives.

Where it begins

It starts in the lining of the cervix

Cervical cancer develops in the cells lining the cervix, almost always after a long-lasting infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). Abnormal changes usually take years to become cancer, which is exactly why regular screening can catch them in time.

Stratified squamous epithelium. Photo: Bioscience Image Library by Fayette Reynolds / Unsplash
Prevention works

A vaccine and a screening test can stop it

The HPV vaccine prevents the infections that cause most cervical cancers, and screening finds precancer early enough to treat. Together they make cervical cancer one of the few cancers that could be eliminated as a public health problem.

Photo: CDC / Unsplash
Access decides

But only if prevention can reach her

When the nearest OB/GYN is an hour away, when a woman has no insurance, or when her county simply has fewer services, prevention that works on paper never arrives. These structural barriers are what this atlas measures.

07 · Methods & data

From public data layers to a county-level picture

The atlas fuses four national data layers into one county-by-county measure of how hard it is for women to get cervical cancer prevention.

How the score is built

Three structural domains, insurance coverage, OB/GYN supply per 10,000 women, and geographic isolation (population density and distance to a large population center), are standardized and averaged with equal, pre-specified weights into a structural access index, expressed as a percentile from 0 to 100. The screening shortfall is how far a county falls below the Healthy People 2030 goal that 84.3% of women are up to date. The combined priority is an equal blend of the two. Double-burden counties are in the worst fifth on both.

Race was never used as an input, and cancer outcomes were never used to build the score.

Checked against real outcomes

In a pre-registered analysis, counties with higher access barriers had higher cervical cancer incidence (Spearman ρ = 0.58) and mortality (ρ = 0.49), and the index added predictive value for mortality beyond the CDC Social Vulnerability Index in states held out of the model. Double-burden counties had about 70% higher cervical cancer incidence than other counties (14.4 vs 8.3 per 100,000).

Atlas vs. the national paper

The atlas maps the contiguous U.S. only (3,109 counties). The companion paper also includes Alaska and Hawaii (3,144 counties). So the paper's national figures are slightly larger: 322 double-burden counties (about 4.8 million women) versus 304 here (about 4.6 million), and 1,446 counties with no OB/GYN versus 1,424 here. All other inputs and data years are identical.

How to read it

  • Scores are relative. A barrier of 80 means higher barriers than about 80% of counties, not an 80% chance of anything.
  • The atlas describes places, not individual women, and is not a measure of anyone's cancer risk.
  • Levels (Low to Severe) are fifths of the national distribution.

Limitations

  • Screening estimates are model-based (CDC PLACES, 2020 survey data).
  • County HPV vaccination data are not available, so vaccination is not in the score.
  • Cancer rates are suppressed for many small counties; Kansas, Minnesota and Virginia do not publish them.
  • Travel time to care is approximated by geographic isolation.
  • Connecticut's new planning regions have no county screening estimates, so they have an access score but no screening or priority score.
  • Alaska and Hawaii are not mapped.

Cervical cancer can be prevented

This atlas describes counties, not individuals, and is not medical advice. If you are a woman looking for care, these resources can help.

HPV vaccination

CDC recommends 2 doses at ages 11–12; vaccination can start at age 9, with catch-up through age 26.

CDC: HPV vaccination

Regular screening

Screening is recommended for most women starting in their twenties. Ask a health care provider which test and schedule are right for you.

CDC: Cervical cancer screening

Free or low-cost screening

CDC's National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program serves women with low incomes and limited insurance.
